Research

I study how quantum systems process information, and what limited access to them lets us learn.

The work is theoretical. Its products are algorithms and lower bounds, stated under explicit assumptions about the dynamics a machine can run, the data it can reach, and the form its answer must take. I care about those assumptions as much as the results: a quantum advantage is a claim about a whole computational model, and it is only as strong as the input and output conventions it rests on.

Two habits give the program its shape. The first is to look for dynamics that already do useful work. Open quantum systems dissipate, filter, and forget; in the right encoding these are computational operations, and they lead to algorithms whose cost can be matched against lower bounds rather than heuristics. The second is to treat access as the primary variable in learning. What a learner can recover about a quantum system depends on the system's structure and on what the learner is allowed to see, and the results worth having come in pairs: a protocol, and a lower bound that shows it cannot be improved by much. The four directions below are expressions of the same point of view.

1

Open-system dynamics as a computational resource

Can dissipation compute?

Closed quantum simulation is unitary: it rotates a state but never contracts it. Open-system evolution, generated by a Lindbladian, is free of that constraint. A Lindbladian can damp, filter, and drive a state toward a target, and those are exactly the operations many numerical problems ask for. This direction asks which computational problems have a natural encoding in open-system dynamics, and what that encoding costs once the input model is made explicit.

The answers so far cover three quite different problems. A general linear differential equation can be embedded in a Lindbladian whose evolution produces the solution, with query complexity that is near-optimal under a plausible input model. Lindbladians that can be fast-forwarded turn out to be powerful enough to run phase estimation at the Heisenberg limit, which connects open-system simulation to one of the standard primitives of quantum algorithms. And a related search-and-sampling algorithm finds rare events, and samples from them, without being told in advance which events are rare.

  1. Dissipation solves linear differential equations.

    General linear differential equations are encoded in Lindbladian dynamics. Under a plausible input model the algorithm has near-optimal dependence on the problem parameters.

    Design nearly optimal quantum algorithm for linear differential equations via Lindbladians · Physical Review Letters, 2025

  2. Fast-forwardable Lindbladians imply phase estimation.

    Fast-forwarding certain Lindbladian processes enables quantum phase estimation at the Heisenberg limit.

    Fast-forwardable Lindbladians imply quantum phase estimation · Quantum Information Processing (QIP) 2026

  3. Rare events can be discovered, not only sampled.

    A quantum algorithm discovers and samples rare events without knowing which events are rare in advance, with optimal scaling in the rarity threshold.

    Quantum enhanced rare event discovery and sampling · arXiv preprint, 2026

Where it goes. The open questions are about the boundary of the resource: which Lindbladians can be fast-forwarded and which cannot, which input models are physically reasonable rather than merely convenient, and how far dissipative encodings extend beyond linear dynamics.

Related: Learning quantum systems from limited access, the inverse problem, where the dynamics are unknown and have to be identified from probes; Quantum linear algebra for machine learning, which supplies much of the toolkit these constructions are built from.

2

Learning quantum systems from limited access

What can a learner recover, and at what cost?

A quantum system is only ever seen through the access one has to it: copies of a state, evolution for a finite time under unknown dynamics, a restricted family of measurements. This direction treats that access as the primary variable. It asks how the structure of a system, its locality, its graphical structure, its purity, changes the cost of learning it, and it looks for answers that come in pairs: a protocol, and a lower bound showing that the protocol cannot be improved by much.

The most recent result is a near-optimal protocol for learning local Lindbladians from finite-time probes. Its cost grows only logarithmically with the size of the system, and matching lower bounds show that both the number of uses of the channel and the total evolution time are essentially necessary. Earlier work established learning guarantees for quantum states close to restricted Boltzmann machines, using exponentially fewer samples than naive tomography, and showed that in the online setting purity does not help: pure and mixed states have the same regret scaling under ℓ1 loss.

  1. Local Lindbladians can be learned near-optimally.

    A protocol learns local Lindbladians from finite-time probes with logarithmic dependence on system size, with matching lower bounds on channel uses and total evolution time.

    Near-Optimal Learning of Local Lindbladians · arXiv preprint, 2026

  2. Graphical structure reduces the cost of learning states.

    For states close to certain restricted Boltzmann machines, provable learning uses exponentially fewer samples than naive tomography.

    Provable learning of quantum states with graphical models · Physical Review Research 8, 013109, 2026

  3. Purity does not make online learning easier.

    Pure and mixed states have the same online regret scaling under ℓ1 loss.

    Online Learning of Pure States is as Hard as Mixed States · Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS) 2025

  4. Importance sampling sharpens operator shadows.

    Importance-sampled operator shadows reduce the measurements needed to estimate selected properties of a state.

    Estimating properties of a quantum state by importance-sampled operator shadows · arXiv preprint, 2023

Where it goes. The natural next questions are what happens when the locality assumption is relaxed, when the noise on the probes is itself unknown, and which of these bounds survive realistic constraints on evolution time.

Related: Open-system dynamics as a computational resource, the forward problem, where a Lindbladian of known form is used as a resource.

Quantum linear algebra for machine learning

When does a speedup survive the model it is applied to?

Quantum computers are efficient at a particular kind of linear algebra: block-encoded matrices, polynomial transformations of their singular values, amplitude estimation. Modern machine learning is linear algebra interleaved with nonlinearity. This direction studies where the two meet, and it tries to keep the accounting honest. A speedup that assumes free quantum access to data, or that ends in a state no one can read out, is not a speedup for the model as it is actually used.

The results locate the regimes in which speedups do survive. The operations inside a Transformer can be expressed with quantum linear-algebra routines, and there are parameter regimes in which inference is faster. Multilayer networks admit a quantum inference method whose advantage over classical evaluation can be characterized. Underneath these sit more basic tools: nonlinear transformations of amplitudes via quantum singular value transformation, and quantum versions of the Alphatron with provable speedups for kernel evaluation. A hands-on tutorial, written with colleagues, collects the models and their assumptions for readers coming from machine learning.

  1. Transformer inference through quantum linear algebra.

    The operations inside a Transformer are expressed with quantum linear-algebra routines, identifying parameter regimes with quantum speedups.

    Quantum Transformer: Accelerating model inference via quantum linear algebra · arXiv preprint, 2024

  2. Quantum inference for multilayer networks.

    A quantum inference method for multilayer neural networks, with an analysis of when it improves on classical evaluation.

    Accelerating Inference for Multilayer Neural Networks with Quantum Computers · International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) 2026

  3. Nonlinear transformations of amplitudes.

    Nonlinear transformations of complex amplitudes constructed via quantum singular value transformation.

    Nonlinear transformation of complex amplitudes via quantum singular value transformation · Physical Review Research 6, 043227, 2024

  4. Quantum Alphatron.

    Quantum versions of the Alphatron algorithm with polynomial speedups for kernel evaluation in specified learning regimes.

    Quantum Alphatron: quantum advantage for learning with kernels and noise · Quantum 7, 1174, 2023

Where it goes. The question I keep returning to is end-to-end cost: what it takes to load the data, run the model, and read out an answer, and whether any regime of practical interest survives all three.

Related: Open-system dynamics as a computational resource, where the same toolkit drives the dissipative encodings.

4

Verifiable AI for research

Can an AI tool make a scientific claim checkable? New direction, started 2026.

Language models now produce fluent scientific arguments, which makes their errors harder to catch, not easier. I think a scientific AI tool should be held to the standard of a paper: each claim traceable to its sources, its assumptions, and the checks that were actually run. This is the newest direction in the program, and it brings the same discipline as the rest, explicit assumptions and verifiable bounds, to AI-assisted research.

I am building a prototype that reconstructs evidence trails and tests reasoning with proofs or benchmarks. It starts with quantum information, where I can judge the output myself.

Where it goes. From a checker for individual claims toward a workspace in which a research question, its literature, and its open sub-problems are maintained together.
